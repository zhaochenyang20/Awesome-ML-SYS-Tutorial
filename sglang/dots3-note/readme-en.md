# dots3-note Preview: What Does an LLM Contestant Do to Take On the IMO?

The word "inference" carries two different meanings. One refers to the process by which an LLM, in order to complete a hard task, gathers feedback from the environment, carries out deep derivation, and decides its next action until it arrives at a final answer (Reasoning). The other is what I spend most of my time on: feeding a prompt into a given LLM and getting back a sampled result (inference). What is rather amusing is that, for a long stretch of time, inference was not particularly aware of Reasoning. Concretely, before roughly 2024, the schools of technique that reasoning relied on, such as chain of thought, tree of thought, and recursive reasoning, all produced outputs that look distinctly short from today's vantage point; and then in January 2025, once DeepSeek made the secret of long context reasoning public, the length of reasoning grew like a monster, which in turn placed real pressure on inference. After that, as everyone has seen, the SGLang ecosystem produced a great many new technical designs for long context reasoning, such as supporting abort / partial rollout for long context reasoning RL, and storing KV cache layer by layer downward in order to hold those terrifying intermediate reasoning results (rationale)...

In August 2025, I finished reading the Kimi K1.5 tech report on a flight to and from SFO, and wrote [Kimi K1.5: A Successful Practice of Long Context RL](../../rlhf/partial-rollout/readme.md) (Chinese only). A year and a half later, the scale of long context has managed to climb yet again. In the K1.5/R1 era, the reasoning everyone discussed took minutes to tens of minutes of decode. And now, on August 14, 2026, RedNote's dots studio open-sourced dots3-note Preview, the model that successfully took on IMO problems. The inference cost they report is this: an ARC-AGI-3 scale complex task requires the Agent to autonomously carry out thousands of rounds of interaction, with a full run taking as long as 40 to 50 hours.

Naturally, a demand like this brings brand-new challenges for Infra.

## How IMO-Scale Math Problems Get Solved

Back in 2025, teams such as Google and OpenAI publicly took on IMO problems for the first time. To make a model guarantee the correctness of a proof, the mainstream approach at the time was formal verification: a human first translates the problem statement into a formal language such as Lean, the model then solves within the formal system, and correctness is guaranteed by Lean's completeness. Naturally, a great many complex problems in the real world cannot be fully formalized, so the generality of this path is fairly limited. (Then again, IMO problems should be exceedingly rare in the real world 😂)

dots this time bypassed formalization entirely. The model directly reads the raw LaTeX input provided by the organizing committee, solves end to end in an agentic manner, and combines natural language reasoning with calls to a Python interpreter (honestly, if we are really arm-wrestling with human contestants, calling a Python interpreter does not seem entirely fair either). Having given up the verifier as a safety net, the correctness of the proof can only be borne by the model itself. The harness the dots team designed is therefore split into three parts: Proof generates candidate proofs, Verify judges whether an existing proof is right or wrong and offers suggestions for improvement, and Refine continues to revise according to Verify's comments; after multiple rounds of parallel reasoning, review, correction, and solution consolidation, the final proof submitted for grading takes shape.

The load shape of this harness is actually rather friendly to inference, and at the very least it aligns nicely with what RadixTree is designed for: the same problem spawns a large number of parallel branches, and those branches share the system prompt, the problem statement, and even the previous several versions of the proof; the Verify stage has to read an entire proof into its context before it can produce a critique; and the Refine stage carries both proof and critique along as it keeps generating. In other words, this is a load that fans out as a tree, shares prefixes heavily, and is dominated by prefill. To make an imprecise analogy, this harness is a modern reimplementation of Tree of Thought.

## TEMPO: Making Self-Evaluation a Training Objective

The Verify stage behaves exactly as we would expect: for any setting that sounds like it could be controlled by a prompt, the results improve significantly once you actually train for it. To strengthen the model's ability to evaluate its own solutions, dots treats "evaluating the current state" as an optimization objective on par with "advancing the task" during the RL stage, and the method is called [TEMPO (Test-Time-Scaled Value Estimation with Macro-Step Policy Optimization)](https://studio.dots.ai/dots/tempo-blog.html).

Ultra-long-horizon RL has two conspicuous difficulties. First, the feedback cycle is too long: the final reward can only be observed once the trajectory ends, and when a single rollout lasts tens of hours, the physical execution time of the task itself becomes the lower bound on how frequently training signals can be obtained. Second, credit assignment is hard: the longer the horizon, the longer the chain of behavior separating early decisions from the final reward, and the harder it becomes to judge which intermediate states genuinely influenced the outcome.

A natural direction is to estimate the future return of the current state before the trajectory has ended. Without waiting for the final outcome, an unfinished trajectory can already produce training signals, which is exactly what actor-critic schemes hope to achieve. But the conventional critic approach bolts a value head onto the model that outputs nothing but a score: feed the current state into the value head, run one forward pass, get a scalar estimate, and you are done. A single estimate from the value head is literally one forward pass, so its compute is fixed; no matter how hard the model's current solving state is, the estimate will not think one step further or spend a bit more compute. The RedNote team points out that **when the actor itself depends on test-time scaling to solve a task, estimating its intermediate states is equally a complex reasoning problem**: the critic needs to review the interaction history, check the hypotheses that have already been formed, judge whether the current search direction is viable, and reason forward about subsequent paths. Therefore, since the actor's capability can grow with test-time compute, the critic used to evaluate these states should have the same property.

### macro-step

With the groundwork above, a very natural idea is to replace the simple value head that previously served as the critic with another generative model, say a smaller reasoning LLM acting as the value head. Once you do that, a single estimate requires generating a fairly long stretch of reasoning, possibly including reflection and tool calls, at a cost far higher than one forward pass of a scalar head, so it cannot possibly run as densely as a conventional critic does after every token or every action.

TEMPO's treatment is to place value estimation at fixed boundaries, defining k consecutive rounds of model-environment interaction as one macro-step, with estimation performed only once at the endpoint. The reason the authors give is that in Agent tasks, meaningful state changes usually occur after reasoning and tool calls have completed and the environment has returned a new observation, so aggregating multiple rounds both leaves the actor room to finish a complete segment of exploration and lets the critic estimate on a state with more sufficient information. (PS: training efficiency is of course another important consideration 😂)

Beyond being the boundary for value estimation, a macro-step is simultaneously the basic unit of rollout and gradient update. GRPO must wait for the entire trajectory to finish and the final reward to arrive before it can compute the reward and perform one gradient update; TEMPO, by contrast, can perform a gradient update as soon as a macro-step ends, collecting the environment rewards within that segment plus the critic's estimate of "how much reward is still left" at the endpoint state, to form the complete reward for this segment, so the actor can be updated without waiting for the task to end.

| | Length of one rollout | Intermediate estimation | How the full horizon is covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| GRPO | Full T rounds | None | Every trajectory runs to completion |
| PPO | Full T rounds | scalar value head, estimated along the way | Every trajectory runs to completion |
| TEMPO | One macro-step, k rounds | generative critic, estimated once at segment end | Save the endpoint state, advance step by step across multiple updates |

For Infra, TEMPO breaks a 40-hour trajectory into several independently schedulable segments, each of which immediately yields training data. This belongs to the same lineage as partial rollout, the only difference being that partial rollout caches the generation state of an unfinished request, whereas TEMPO saves the joint state of the actor and the environment, and such an intermediate joint state can be used for training.

### Generative Critic and Privileged Information

The critic's input is the current macro-step endpoint state, plus the interaction records from this segment and earlier; its output is an entire reasoning rationale with a numerical estimate appended at the end. Its reasoning process tries to answer three questions: which regularities of the environment the actor has figured out so far, what solution it is currently attempting, and which obstacles remain unresolved.

What is rather interesting is that the critic can also read things the actor cannot see at all, such as the environment's internal state, hidden rules, test plans, and even the game source code. This privileged information enters only the critic's context and never the actor's, and the reason is plain enough: once the actor can read the source code, it no longer needs to explore, and the entire training objective would immediately degenerate into "copying the answer." Note that when the actor is deployed online after training to sit the final IMO exam, the critic does not participate at all, so it is reasonable to give the critic information beyond what the actor gets; it is only responsible for judging whether the actor's direction is right, and one more piece of ground truth only makes that judgment more accurate.

In my own research career, it seems to me that judging much work is more complex than doing the work itself. For objective tasks, such as inference systems, the criteria are extremely objective, and we have plenty of widely known metrics such as TTFT and TBT; but this world also contains a great many subjective tasks that are "indescribable and unnameable," such as whether someone's code is written to standard, whether someone is dependable within a team, or whether the proof method dots3-note used on an IMO problem is scientific and rigorous. Unfortunately, the more subjective the task, the higher the cost of evaluation tends to be, as far as I can tell. But there are always shortcuts for subjective evaluation, such as allowing the evaluating side to use information the generating side cannot obtain. The critic is precisely exploiting an asymmetry with the actor to carry out evaluation, and this asymmetry is not one of capability but one of information.

As for how to train a generative critic model, that is a question the value head era never had to consider. Training a value head is standard regression: given a state and a target value, compute MSE, and the gradient flows back into the few layers of the value head, clean and tidy. But a generative critic outputs an entire stretch of tokens, of which only the numerical value at the end counts as the target, while the thousands of tokens of reasoning before it carry no supervision signal at all. If you regress directly on that final number, the gradient essentially acts only on the last few tokens. That is not quite our objective; what we want to train is the critic's ability to arrive at an accurate value through reasoning, so supervision on the reasoning process matters enormously.

TEMPO's solution is to treat value fitting itself as an RL problem as well, with exactly the same idea as GRPO. Concretely, first draw m independent estimates for the same state, each yielding an entire stretch of reasoning plus a numerical value; then assign each sample a reward according to the error between that number and the target value V, the smaller the error the higher the reward; next perform within-group centering over these m rewards to obtain the advantage of each sample; and finally update by policy gradient, so the gradient naturally lands on the whole stretch of reasoning. The critic is rewarded again and again, and learns a better way of thinking.

### The Actor Doubles as the Critic

Given that the critic is a generative model that can reason, call tools, and even read source code, should we train it separately? In the TEMPO scheme, the actor simply doubles as the critic: while running a macro-step the model is the actor, and at the segment-end boundary it switches to a different prompt and takes in extra environment evaluation information, whereupon the same set of weights plays the critic.

Of course, this design harbors a potential conflict. The previous section discussed how the critic can read extra environment information while the actor cannot, and now the two roles are in fact the same set of parameters, so will the actor indirectly learn the answers through shared weights and cheat in the exam? We can think about this from two angles, context management and parameter sharing. As long as the environment information appears only in the critic's context, where its purpose is to check whether the actor's direction is right, then when the model's turn comes to act as the actor, its context contains none of these things, and there is no cheating to speak of. Going further, gradient updates on the critic side do land on the same set of parameters, and as to whether the rules themselves get incidentally memorized into the parameters during training, candidly the original text does not make a judgment, nor have we seen an experiment that would falsify it. Judging by results, the model this shared-weight scheme ultimately produced is solid in capability and robust in real-world generalization, so even if latent leakage exists, it has not caused any substantive problem.

> Note: On whether sharing parameters between the critic and the actor amounts, in some sense, to letting the actor cheat, I had a brief chat with Qiao Chao from the RedNote technical team. Their experimental observation is roughly this: long-horizon tasks consume prompts extremely slowly; for a 2048-round task with a 64-step macro-step, a single data point takes a long time to be used up, and training rarely finishes even one epoch. Privileged information is only the critic's context for that one inference, and if the data is trained across multiple batches, the model is unlikely to memorize the shortcut into its parameters. In short, the potential downside of sharing critic and actor parameters is relatively contained.

Setting that concern aside, the positive argument for parameter sharing is that the two kinds of task draw on the same underlying understanding. The actor needs to track environment changes, maintain its own hypotheses, and plan the next action, while the critic needs to judge how far progress has come, which hypotheses might be wrong, and whether any viable path remains, and what these two things require at bottom essentially overlaps. Once shared, the environmental perception the actor accumulates through interaction can be taken straight over for value estimation, while the sense of "this way is blocked" that the critic acquires over a great many intermediate states flows back into the actor along the same set of parameters.

Landing on Infra, this design has an upside and a downside: the good news is that there is no need to feed another critic model, and the rollout engine still serves the same set of weights with requests merely split into two kinds of prompt; the bad news is that these two kinds of request have quite different load shapes, since the actor side is multi-turn interaction with long context and short output, emitting only hundreds to thousands of tokens per turn, whereas the critic side is one-shot reasoning with ultra-long context and long output, generating in one go a large stretch of analysis containing reflection and tool calls. During training, the inference scheduler has to accommodate both shapes within the same batch, and the benefit of continuous batching gets pulled down quite a bit.

> Note: In fact, according to the RedNote team's feedback, training is serial: critic and actor requests do not end up in a mixed batch.

### The Policy Gradient of a macro-step

Note that TEMPO performs a gradient update on only one macro-step at a time, so is it still optimizing the original objective of "the expected return of the whole trajectory"? If not, then all the designs above are merely optimizing some surrogate objective, and good results would be by practice rather than by design.

The dots team gives an argument in the appendix. The idea is to partition the full trajectory uniquely into M consecutive macro-steps under a fixed rule, noting that this partition merely regroups the same set of state-action pairs without adding or removing any of them, so the policy gradient of the full trajectory can be rewritten and reorganized macro-step by macro-step as-is, and after rearranging the conclusion is:

**Under a fixed partition rule and a fixed number M of macro-steps, the policy gradient of the full trajectory is equivalent to: uniformly select one macro-step, compute the gradient of that macro-step alone, and multiply by M.**

This equivalence holds under one precondition, namely that the sampled macro-step and the prefix preceding it must both come from the current actor. And TEMPO's continuation mechanism violates exactly this, because the prefix saved as a starting point was generated several rounds earlier by an older version of the actor, so the state distribution of the starting point has already drifted from the distribution the current actor would itself reach. The dots team's treatment is to apply an importance-sampling correction to the prefix, that is, to multiply the sample by a weight of "the probability the new policy reaches this prefix divided by the probability the old policy reaches this prefix," pulling the distribution back into place.

It is worth mentioning that K1.5's partial rollout faces exactly the same problem: the cached portion of a trajectory was generated by the old policy, and after restoration it continues under the new policy, which strictly speaking is no longer on-policy. Kimi's treatment at the time was relatively naive, resting mainly on the empirical judgment that "the deviation is not large" (by practice), whereas TEMPO pushes the scale to a trajectory that may span a dozen-odd parameter updates, where the deviation is too large to be papered over by experience, and a formal correction becomes mandatory (by design).

### Experimental Results

The dots team took 25 games from the ARC-AGI-3 public set and ran each model twice on each game, comparing TEMPO, a GRPO model trained from the same starting point, and a base checkpoint without RL. Score here measures both task progress and action efficiency: advancing to a deeper level earns a higher score, while under the premise of reaching the same progress, the fewer environment interactions consumed the higher the score.

Under a budget of at most 2048 interactions, GRPO shows a considerable improvement over base, indicating that RL with short rollouts and no value model already yields gains on its own; TEMPO improves over base by 31.5% and by a further 20.6% on top of GRPO; and once level pass rates are aligned, TEMPO's Score is still higher. That last point is in fact more convincing than the second, because a higher score could still be explained by "exploring more," whereas using fewer steps at the same progress cannot be explained that way, and it shows the model really does judge earlier which paths lead nowhere, which is to say the critic's judgment really has generalized back into the actor.

As for absolute position, viewed through the harness of the official evaluation code:

| Model | ARC-AGI-3 (arcagi3 harness) | ARC-AGI-2 |
|---|---|---|
| dots3-note Preview | 6.9 | 81.4 |
| Claude Opus 4.8 | 1.5 | 72.1 |
| GPT-5.5 | 0.4 | 85.0 |

6.9 is of course a very low absolute number, but it is already more than four times the runner-up, and it bears noting that ARC-AGI-3 examines whether a model can learn autonomously in an unfamiliar environment while ARC-AGI-2 examines static abstract reasoning, two entirely different capabilities, which is why you get a combination in which dots3 does not lead GPT-5.5 on ARC-AGI-2 yet opens up an order of magnitude on ARC-AGI-3.

## The Infra Setup for an IMO Contestant

The designs mentioned at the outset, whether abort, partial rollout, or storing KV cache layer by layer downward, all share an implicit premise that was never written down, namely that **the context of a single reasoning trajectory always fits within the model's attention window**. However long R1-era reasoning got, several hundred thousand tokens of rationale still sat within context length, and what engineering had to solve was insufficient GPU memory, not the need to trim the attention window to match. At the scale of IMO-class problems, the 40-to-50-hour agent with thousands of rounds of interaction breaks that premise. Once task length exceeds the window, history must be trimmed (called compact in Claude Code and Codex), and the problems trimming causes are not quite the same as running out of memory; as discussed later, when the context window is insufficient, an approach like HiCache that stores KV cache at a lower tier simply does not exist as an option. To describe the changes this process brings, let us unfold the discussion step by step.

### Memory Footprint of the Dots Note Model

Before getting into the compact discussion, let us directly compute the memory footprint of the dots model. dots3's `max_position_embeddings` is 524288, that is 512K, a window that already ranks among the longest in today's open-source models. [config.json](https://huggingface.co/dots-studio/dots3-note-prev/blob/main/config.json) states that the model has 46 layers in total, of which 13 are full attention and 33 are sliding window. Although both kinds of layer are MLA, their parameter configurations are not the same:

| | full layer (13 layers) | SWA layer (33 layers) |
|---|---|---|
| `kv_lora_rank` | 512 | 1024 |
| `qk_nope_head_dim` | 128 | 192 |
| `num_attention_heads` | 128 | 64 |
| `rope_theta` | 8e7 | 5e4 |
| sliding window | — | 513 |
| DSA indexer | yes, `index_topk=2048` | no |

Under MLA, the KV bytes to store per token per layer is `(kv_lora_rank + qk_rope_head_dim) × dtype_size`, entirely independent of head count, because what MLA writes out is the compressed latent vector, while the matrix that expands the latent back into each head's K and V is a fixed weight of the layer, computed on the fly every forward pass and never entering the cache. Plugging in the numbers, under BF16 a full layer is 1152 bytes, plus 132 bytes for the DSA index key and its quantization scale, for a total of 1284 bytes, while an SWA layer is 2176 bytes. Multiplying these two numbers by layer count and sequence length, the KV a request that fills the full 512K requires is:

| Hypothetical architecture | KV per request | Relative to dots3 |
|---|---|---|
| Standard MHA (128 heads, head_dim 128) | 1472 GiB | 180x |
| All 46 layers on full MLA + DSA | 28.8 GiB | 3.5x |
| dots3's hybrid (13 full + 33 SWA) | 8.19 GiB | 1x |

Two things in this table are worth noting. First, the 33 SWA layers account for only 40.6 MiB in total, and it is a constant, because once the 513 window is aligned to 576 tokens under page 64, it becomes completely independent of how long the sequence actually is; converted over, 99.5% of the 8.19 GiB comes from those 13 full layers. Second, this table is basically the entire answer to why a 512K context is feasible: under standard MHA a single request needs 1.4 TiB and one H200 cannot hold even one, whereas MLA compresses the width from a function of head count into a single hyperparameter and hybrid SWA turns the capacity of 33 layers from growing with the sequence into a constant, and only after stacking the two does it land at the 8 GiB scale that can actually be engineered.

Following this number down to concurrency, 8 H200s at 141 GiB each give a static pool of about 122.7 GiB per card under `--mem-fraction-static 0.87`; the 537 GiB of BF16 weights spread across TP8/EP8 comes to 67.1 GiB per card; leaving another 8 to 12 GiB for CUDA graph and activations, 43 to 47 GiB per card remains for KV. Given that SGLang's deployment recipe recommends enabling `--enable-dp-attention --dp-size 8`, attention runs data-parallel and a request's KV lands entirely on one rank without being sharded, so each card can hold roughly 5 requests filling the full 512K, and eight ranks together give 43 to 46. Admittedly this does not even account for prefix reuse in the KV cache, which is a rather unreasonable simplification. From this we can see that a single request at 512K context length is quite comfortable on H200. That said, a model's context window is never determined by how much the KV cache can hold; it is a rather complex parameter that depends on the extrapolation range of the position embedding and on how long the sequences seen during training actually were, among other things.

### Trimming and Cache Invalidation

Unfortunately, the 512K window is still not enough for tasks at the scale of ARC-AGI-3. The number dots gives is that a complex task requires the Agent to autonomously carry out thousands of rounds of interaction, with a full run taking as long as 40 to 50 hours; at a rough few thousand tokens per round, the cumulative context easily reaches the scale of several million tokens, which exceeds 512K by considerably more than one order of magnitude. As for how the IMO harness actually manages context, the official text mentions only "letting the Agent recursively generate proofs and, through tool calls, self-evaluate and enhance the generated proofs," without disclosing the specifics. That said, the NL2repo evaluation configuration in the blog appendix can serve as a reference point, being the long-horizon agentic setup closest to this shape that they have made public: each task is capped at 250 turns and 10 hours, with 4 CPU cores and 32 GB of RAM, and inference uses temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, a maximum output of 49152 tokens, and a 384K context window; and when the context limit is exceeded, older reasoning and overly long tool inputs and outputs are trimmed, while the latest 24 messages and complete tool-call / result pairs are preserved.

> Note: This is the official NL2repo harness configuration, and has nothing to do with TEMPO. TEMPO's macro-step treats the sandbox as a black box; how it truncates and how it compresses context is defined by the harness inside that box. The scheme above of "dropping earlier reasoning and keeping only the most recent few rounds" is truncation in essence, not the kind of summarize Claude Code does by writing a memory, and it also has no direct relation to the harness used when training the dots model.

Before trimming is triggered, agentic load is actually rather friendly to the engine. The prompt of round i fully contains the prompt of round i-1, and radix cache is a prefix tree, so this pattern of only growing at the back while leaving the front untouched achieves a hit rate close to 100%, and all that genuinely needs prefilling each round is the small newly added segment, namely the tool return plus the model's output for that round, on the order of a few thousand tokens.

Once trimming is triggered, however, the impact on KV cache reuse is quite pronounced. Trimming the earlier reasoning means a stretch in the middle of the sequence gets carved out, and the positions of all subsequent tokens shift forward; and since RoPE encodes absolute position, once the position changes the previous KV cache is simply unusable. Trimming necessarily comes with a re-prefill of the entire context, and this is something no tier of cache can change. This is also where the demands the two generations of reasoning models place on the inference system diverge: in the R1 era, a large volume of concurrent requests would push KV cache past the memory limit, and we chose to offload storage tier by tier downward; whereas truncation/compact means KV cache invalidation, and re-prefill inevitably happens.

Taking a 384K context window, 3000 new tokens per round (tool return plus model output), and roughly 75% retained after trimming, the first trigger comes around round 130, after which it triggers roughly every 32 rounds, each time requiring a re-prefill of about 295K tokens; amortized per round, average prefill rises from 3000 tokens to about 12200, which is 4.1x the no-trimming case. These assumptions are of course not necessarily accurate, but the order of magnitude of the conclusion is stable: the cost curve of a long trajectory is not flat, but rather segments of cheap incremental decode repeatedly interrupted by periodic large-scale re-prefill.

> Note: On the wait time of compact, an obvious direction is looking-forward compact: once it looks likely that the next tool call will blow the window, the server can finish compact in advance and overlap it with the next generation, hiding the re-prefill wait.

### How Model Architecture Affects Recomputation Cost

Recomputation cannot be dodged, but dots3's architecture in fact makes it considerably cheaper than one might imagine. Let us take a single 295K-token re-prefill as reference and compute the floating-point operations in its attention portion:

| | attention compute | share |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothetical 46 layers all dense | 58.4 PFLOP | — |
| dots3 actual | 5.85 PFLOP | 100% |
| 　of which 33 SWA layers | | 2.1% |
| 　of which 13 DSA indexer layers | | 79.2% |
| 　of which 13 DSA main attention layers | | 18.7% |

The reason for saving this factor of ten is not hard to see: each query in the 33 SWA layers considers only 513 tokens, giving complexity `O(L·W)` rather than `O(L²)`; and the main attention of the 13 full layers is compressed by DSA down to `O(L·topk)`, with each query looking at only 2048 tokens, equally inexpensive.

What is genuinely worth chewing on is the composition of what remains, where nearly eighty percent of the cost falls on DSA's indexer. The reason is direct enough: the indexer must first use low-dimensional heads to compute relevance scores between the current query and every historical token before it can pick out the top-k, so this step remains thoroughly `O(L²)`, merely using 64 heads of 128 dimensions, about three times cheaper than main attention's 128 heads of 192 dimensions. In other words, DSA cut away main attention's quadratic term but introduced a new quadratic term of its own, only with a smaller coefficient; if one wanted to do kernel-level optimization for a load like re-prefill, what should clearly be touched is the indexer's top-k selection step, not the already-cheap main attention.


### Stability and Deployment

For an inference run that has to last 40 to 50 hours, the most basic requirement on the serving side is stability, and several settings in the [SGLang Cookbook](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/6ad3f2d8fdc8b0b0411746ef6f77f731b0339541/docs/cookbook/autoregressive/RedNote/Dots3-Note.mdx) are quite telling. `--watchdog-timeout 1800` and `SGLANG_WARMUP_TIMEOUT=1800` are both several times the defaults, because a 295K-token recomputation stacked on top of DeepEP's all-to-all makes single-step latency far exceed ordinary loads, and a watchdog set too tight will easily kill legitimate requests. `--cuda-graph-backend-prefill disabled` keeps prefill out of graph, partly because variable-length prefill is ill-suited to graph in the first place, and partly because this model's SWA layers take a prefill path that requires slicing each request's sequence tail on the host side, which cannot enter graph either. `--cuda-graph-backend-decode full` paired with `--cuda-graph-max-bs-decode 32`, where that 32 is precisely the same order of magnitude as the 43 concurrent requests computed earlier, shows that the graph's coverage was set against real concurrency rather than filled in with a default. The script also downgrades automatically by memory: on cards below 120 GiB, if `--language-only` is not enabled, it simply turns the decode graph off, because graph memory and the KV pool compete for the same budget.

Incidentally, the merged cookbook documentation states plainly that H100 cannot run the BF16 version: 8 cards total 640 GiB, giving a static pool of about 557 GiB under `--mem-fraction-static 0.87`, while the BF16 weights alone are 537 GiB, leaving no usable KV pool at all at a 524288 context, so H100 users are pointed straight at FP8. Leaving a configuration that does not work in the documentation and showing you the arithmetic is far more useful than quietly deleting it. For the detailed method behind this kind of memory arithmetic, see [What Exactly Is SGLang OOM-ing On?](../kvcache-code-walk-through/mem-fraction-static-en.md).
